Variational autoencoders are a clean example of how a generative model can pair a hidden representation with a tractable training objective. They assume that each observation `x` is produced from an unobserved latent variable `z`, then learn both a decoder that maps `z` back to data and an encoder that approximates the posterior distribution over `z`.

<Callout type="tip" title="Useful mental model">
A VAE is not just an autoencoder with noise. It is a latent-variable model where the encoder exists to make likelihood-based training practical.
</Callout>

## Model

The generative story is:

1. Draw a latent variable `z` from a simple prior, usually $p(z) = N(0, I)$.
2. Draw data from a decoder distribution $p_\theta(x \mid z)$.

The challenge is that the exact posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ is usually intractable. VAEs introduce an encoder $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ that acts as a learned approximation.

<TwoColumns>
<Fragment slot="left">

### Encoder

The encoder maps an observation to a distribution over latents, typically a diagonal Gaussian with learned mean and variance. Its job is not to reconstruct directly; it proposes where in latent space the observation likely came from.

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="right">

### Decoder

The decoder maps a latent sample back to a distribution over observations. Sampling from the prior and decoding is what turns the trained model into a generator rather than only a compressor.

</Fragment>
</TwoColumns>

## Objective

<Statement label="Definition" title="evidence lower bound" id="def:vae-elbo" accent="accent">
For one observation $x$, latent prior $p(z)$, decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z)$, and variational posterior $q_\phi(z \mid x)$,
$$
\log p_\theta(x)
\ge
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]
-
\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z \mid x) \| p(z)).
$$
The right-hand side is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO.
</Statement>

<Proof>
Start from
$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=
\log \int p_\theta(x \mid z)p(z)\,dz.
$$
Multiply and divide the integrand by $q_\phi(z \mid x)$, then view the result as an expectation under $q_\phi(z \mid x)$. Jensen's inequality moves the logarithm inside the expectation, producing the reconstruction term minus the KL penalty.
</Proof>

<Box title="Training loss">
Implementations usually minimize the negative ELBO:
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x)
=
-\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]
+
\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z \mid x) \| p(z)).
$$
</Box>

The first term rewards reconstructions that explain the observed data. The second term keeps the inferred latents close to the prior, which makes it possible to sample new examples by drawing `z` from the prior and decoding it.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not over-read the latent axes">
A smooth latent space is encouraged, not guaranteed. Without the right inductive biases or diagnostics, individual coordinates can mix factors in ways that look interpretable but are not stable.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Where the approximation enters">
The ELBO is a lower bound because $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ may not match the exact posterior. Better variational families tighten the bound, but they can also make optimization and sampling more expensive.
</Callout>

## Why it matters

VAEs are useful as a template for probabilistic representation learning. They make the trade-off between compression, reconstruction, and sampleability explicit, and they give a compact language for discussing posterior approximation, regularized latent spaces, and generative modeling.
