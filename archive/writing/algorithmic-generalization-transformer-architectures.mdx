## TL;DR

What architectural mechanisms are needed to enable transformers to generalize algorithmically beyond their training distribution? We find that standard transformers, even with Chain-of-Thought training, struggle to scale their reasoning to more complex problems. We explore four key mechanisms which we hypothesize are key to robust algorithmic generalization: ***(i)* input-adaptive recurrence**, ***(ii)* incremental computation schedules**, ***(iii)* anchored latent representations via a discrete bottleneck**, and ***(iv)* an explicit error-correction mechanism**. When combined, these mechanisms enable transformers to develop genuine algorithmic reasoning that generalizes to problems much larger than those seen during training. We further show that a model can discover its own computation schedule, without supervision on when each node should be computed, and that the schedule it discovers closely tracks circuit depth.{/* Our mechanistic analysis further uncovers *how* these models compute, revealing a learned circuit that performs modular arithmetic using induction heads and frequency-domain operations. */}

## Motivation: Do Language Models Truly Reason?

The reasoning capabilities of **Large Language Models (LLMs)** have advanced dramatically in recent years, particularly through **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)** techniques that enable models to generate step-by-step reasoning traces. These advances have led to impressive performance on mathematical reasoning tasks, with models now solving complex problems that once seemed beyond reach.

But beneath this impressive performance lies a fundamental question: Do LLMs truly learn to implement **scalable *algorithms*** and **reason *systematically***, or do they simply **memorize patterns and heuristics** that happen to work within their training distribution?

Recent evidence points toward the latter. Even the largest models often fail when tested on longer or more complex inputs ([Anil et al. 2022](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/fb7451e43f9c1c35b774bcfad7a5714b-Abstract-Conference.html); [Jelassi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15400); [Mirzadeh et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06941)). Performance collapses abruptly once tasks exceed the length or complexity of the training regime, indicating that models may learn distribution-specific shortcuts rather than true, compositional algorithms.

This motivates the central question explored in this work:

<Callout type="question" title="The Central Question">
What architectural mechanisms are needed to enable transformer models to discover robust and scalable algorithmic solutions that can generalize beyond the training distribution to larger and more complex problems?
</Callout>

## Our Testbed: Modular Arithmetic on Scalable Computation Graphs

### The Task Definition

**Problem:** Evaluate arithmetic expressions on **directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)** under modular arithmetic (modulo 23).

- **Input:** A computation graph where:
  - **Leaf nodes** are assigned integer values
  - **Internal nodes** are computed via arithmetic operations on their dependencies
  - All operations ($+, -, \times $) performed modulo 23

- **Output:** Compute the values of all nodes in the graph

### A Concrete Example

Consider this computation graph with 9 nodes:

<Diagram figure="algorithmic-generalization-transformer-architectures/computation-graph" alt="Computation Graph">
  <Fragment slot="caption">Computation graph illustration: green nodes ($x_7$, $x_{42}$, $x_{88}$, $x_{115}$) are leaf nodes with given values; pink boxes denote arithmetic operations ($+$, $\times$, $-$); blue nodes ($x_{23}$, $x_{91}$, $x_{55}$, $x_{101}$, $x_{30}$) are computed from their dependencies.</Fragment>
</Diagram>

<Callout type="info" title="The Task">
Given $x_7=20$, $x_{42}=2$, $x_{88} = 6$, $x_{115} =14$, compute $x_{23}$, $x_{91}$, $x_{55}$, $x_{101}$, and $x_{30}$
</Callout>

### Input Representation

The computation graph is presented to the model as a **token sequence**.

**Token Vocabulary:**

- **Values**: `0`, `1`, ..., `22` (mod 23)
- **Variables**: `x_0`, `x_1`, ..., `x_127`
- **Operations**: `+`, `-`, `×`
- **Special tokens**: `→` (assignment), `[sep]` (separator)

**Example Token Sequence for the graph above:**

<Box title="Example Token Sequence">
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\langle 20 \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_7 \rangle [\text{sep}]
 \langle 2 \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{42} \rangle [\text{sep}]
 \langle 6 \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{88} \rangle [\text{sep}]
 \langle 14 \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{115} \rangle [\text{sep}] \\
&\langle x_7 \rangle \langle + \rangle \langle x_{42} \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{23} \rangle [\text{sep}] \langle x_{42} \rangle \langle + \rangle \langle x_{88} \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{91} \rangle [\text{sep}] \langle x_{88} \rangle \langle \times \rangle \langle x_{115} \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{55} \rangle [\text{sep}] \\
&\langle x_{23} \rangle \langle \times \rangle \langle x_{91} \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{101} \rangle [\text{sep}] \langle x_{91} \rangle \langle - \rangle \langle x_{88} \rangle \langle + \rangle \langle x_{55} \rangle \langle \to \rangle \langle x_{30} \rangle
\end{aligned}
$$
</Box>

This sequence serves as the **input prompt** to the model.

### The Natural Algorithm

To solve this problem, a natural algorithmic approach is to **compute nodes layer-by-layer in topological order**:

**Iteration 1 (Depth 1)** - Compute nodes that depend only on leaf values:

- $x_{23} = x_7 + x_{42} = 20 + 2 = 22 \pmod{23}$
- $x_{91} = x_{42} + x_{88} = 2 + 6 = 8 \pmod{23}$
- $x_{55} = x_{88} \times x_{115} = 6 \times 14 = 15 \pmod{23}$

**Iteration 2 (Depth 2)** - Compute nodes that depend on depth ≤ 1:

- $x_{101} = x_{23} \times x_{91} = 22 \times 8 = 15 \pmod{23}$
- $x_{30} = x_{91} - x_{88} + x_{55} = 8 - 6 + 15 = 17 \pmod{23}$

<Callout title="Key Property: Depth-Invariant Algorithm">
The **complexity** of each problem is parameterized by graph size $N$ and depth $D$. However, the **algorithm itself is independent of depth** each step—it is a recursive procedure with a shared layer-by-layer procedure!

**The Critical Test for Algorithmic Learning:**

- If a model learns and can execute a scalable ***algorithm***, it should be able to generalize to graphs of any size
- If it memorizes **patterns**, performance will collapse on larger graphs

This setup provides a controlled environment for probing whether Transformers can truly learn *scalable algorithms*.
</Callout>


### Why This is an Ideal Testbed for Algorithmic Generalization

This task possesses three key properties that make it perfect for testing whether models learn genuine algorithms:

1. **Complexity is Parameterized by Graph Size**: Problem complexity is directly controlled by the number of nodes $N$ and depth $D$ in the graph.

2. **Target Algorithm is Depth-Invariant**: The layer-by-layer algorithm that solves the problem has a **recursive compositional structure**. Thus, once the core problem-solving procedure is learned, it can generalize to arbitrary inputs by the number of iterations, enabling algorithmic generalization.

3. **Captures Real Mathematical Reasoning**: This task mirrors the structure of established mathematical reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K, where multi-step arithmetic must be performed in dependency order.

**The Critical Test**: If a model learns the **algorithm** (layer-by-layer traversal), it should work on graphs of any size. If it memorizes **distribution-specific patterns**, performance will collapse on larger graphs.

## Experimental Setup & Baseline Methods

### Training and Testing Data

**Training:** Models are trained on randomly generated computation graphs with graph size limited by $N \leq 32$.

**Testing for Algorithmic Generalization:** To assess algorithmic generalization, we vary graph size up to $N = 128$, inspecting the degradation of performance of different methods as complexity scales beyond the training regime.

These larger problem instances are not only longer (i.e., input representation scales with graph size), but also have larger *computational depth*, requiring computation to scale beyond anything seen during training. That is, generalizing to these more complex problem instances requires an ability to **handle longer inputs** as well as an ability to **scale computation beyond the training regime**.

### Baseline Methods

To establish the limitations of current approaches, we evaluate two standard training paradigms:

#### 1. **End-to-End Training**

Standard transformer models trained to directly output all node values given the input, without explicit intermediate steps.

- **Input:** Token sequence representing the graph (as shown above)
- **Output:** Direct prediction of all node values
- **Architectures tested:** Both feedforward and recurrent transformers

#### 2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Training**

The prevalent technique for multi-step reasoning in LLMs. Instead of directly outputting the answer, CoT trains the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps.

- **Input:** Token sequence representing the graph followed by a special $\langle \text{CoT} \rangle$ token indicating the start of the CoT trace
- **Output:** Step-by-step computation in topological order

**Example CoT output for computing $x_{101}$:**
$$
[\text{...Input Prompt...}] \langle \text{CoT} \rangle [\text{...}] \langle x_{101} \rangle = \langle x_{23} \rangle \langle \times \rangle \langle x_{91} \rangle = \langle 22 \rangle \langle \times \rangle \langle 8 \rangle = \langle 15 \rangle
$$

Here, `[...]` denotes the preceding CoT trajectory that computed $x_{23}$ and $x_{91}$.

**Implementation:** For all methods, we conduct extensive hyperparameter search (layers, model dimension, positional encoding) and select the best-performing configuration for comparison.

### Observed OOD Generalization Deficiencies

<Callout type="warning" title="Key Findings: Catastrophic Failure Beyond Training Distribution">
- **End-to-End models** (both feedforward and recurrent): Fail to effectively learn the task even in-distribution, with performance rapidly degrading as graph size increases.

- **Chain-of-Thought** enables significant improvement, achieving near-perfect in-distribution performance ($N \leq 32$). However, it exhibits only **limited OOD generalization** to moderately larger graphs ($N \approx 48$), and this capability **rapidly deteriorates** as graph sizes exceed the training regime.

**Key Takeaway:** Even CoT—with its explicit step-by-step supervision—learns distribution-specific shortcuts rather than a generalizable algorithm. The token-based linear reasoning format proves brittle and fails to scale.
</Callout>

**These failures highlight a clear need for architectural mechanisms that move beyond brittle token-level reasoning towards models with a native ability to learn algorithms.**

### Our Solution: Four Architectural Mechanisms

Effective out-of-distribution generalization demands more than memorizing reasoning traces—it requires learning a scalable algorithm that can adapt its computation to the input’s complexity. We identify four key mechanisms that together enable Transformers to learn true algorithmic reasoning:

<Callout type="info" title="Core Principle: Depth-Invariant Latent Space Reasoning">
Rather than forcing computation into a token-by-token format (like CoT), we enable **native latent-space reasoning** through:

1. **Recurrence & Adaptive Computation** - Scale computation time with problem complexity via recurrence
2. **Incremental Computation Schedules** - Learn a *computation schedule*, an assignment of each part of the computation to a recurrent iteration, via latent space supervision
3. **Discrete Latent Anchoring** - Prevent representational drift across iterations via discretization
4. **Self-Correction** - Detect and recover from intermediate errors

These mechanisms impose a **depth-invariant structure**: the model learns a recursive solution to the problem, enabling robust scaling far beyond training depth.
</Callout>

### Detailed Mechanism Descriptions

<Callout title="Mechanism 1: Recurrence & Input-Adaptive Computation" accent="accent">
**Motivation**: Systematic generalization to more complex problem instances requires the ability to scale computation time proportionate to input complexity, beyond the training regime.

**Implementation**: We employ a recurrent Transformer block that iteratively processes the input:

$$
(E_1^{(t+1)}, \ldots, E_n^{(t+1)}) \gets \mathrm{RecurrentTransformerBlock}(E_1^{(t)}, \ldots, E_n^{(t)}), \quad t = 1, 2, \ldots, T
$$

Crucially, the number of recurrent iterations $T$ is not fixed—it **adapts to the input complexity**. Specifically, $T$ scales linearly with the depth $D$ of the computation graph. This input-adaptive recurrence enables dynamic scaling of computation time to match problem complexity.

**Key Advantage**: Unlike CoT methods that scale computation by generating progressively longer token sequences, recurrence introduces inductive biases favoring recursive solution structures that are inherently more scalable.

</Callout>

<Callout title="Mechanism 2: Learning an Incremental Computation Schedule" accent="accent">
**Motivation**: While a recurrent architecture provides the *capacity* for iterative computation, it does not provide an explicit mechanism for *learning* what computation is performed in each step. When only the final answer is supervised, the model may divide the work across iterations arbitrarily. To successfully generalize out-of-distribution to deeper, more complex inputs, the model must learn a robust **computation schedule**: an assignment of each part of the computation to a recurrent iteration. Specifically, we seek computation schedules that enforce incremental **sparse updates**. At each iteration, the model modifies only a subset of the output state and leaves the remainder unchanged, so that it progressively builds upon the solution computed so far. Crucially, the computational complexity of each step remains bounded and does not grow with problem size.

**Implementation**: We learn such a schedule through a **latent space supervision** approach, where a loss is applied to the model's intermediate latent states after every recurrent iteration, rather than applying a loss only to the final output. We consider two ways to learn the schedule. In *Mechanism 2a*, the schedule is supervised during training using the depth of each node. In *Mechanism 2b*, the model discovers its own schedule without supervision on when each node should be computed.

</Callout>

<Callout title="Mechanism 2a: Depth-Supervised Scheduling" accent="accent">
**Implementation**: The layer-by-layer algorithm defines a natural computation schedule to target. At each iteration, the algorithm computes every node whose parents have all been computed, so that iteration $d+1$ computes the values of all nodes at depth $d$. At each recurrent iteration $t$, we use a shared *linear readout layer* to predict node values from the latent embeddings $E_i^{(t)}$ and apply a step-wise loss that aligns the model with the target layer-by-layer algorithm:

$$
\mathrm{DepthSupervisedLoss} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{i \in [n]} \ell\big(W_{\mathtt{value}} \, E_i^{(t)},\ \mathrm{Mask}\big(\mathrm{Value}(x_i),\ \mathrm{Depth}(x_i) < t\big)\big)
$$

where $\mathrm{Value}(x_i)$ is the ground-truth value of $x_i$, $W_{\mathtt{value}}$ is the shared readout, $\ell$ is the cross-entropy loss, and $\mathrm{Mask}(v, b)$ returns $v$ when $b$ holds and $\langle \mathtt{empty} \rangle$ otherwise, a special value that marks a node as not yet computed. $\mathrm{Depth}(x_i)$ is the node depth in the circuit, defined recursively: leaf nodes have depth $0$, and every other node has depth one greater than the maximum depth of its parents.

**Intuition**: The loss requires that after iteration $t$, the model has computed the values of all nodes with depth less than $t$ and marked all other nodes as $\langle \mathtt{empty} \rangle$. For example, in the graph above, supervision at $t=1$ applies to leaf nodes (e.g., $x_7$), while at $t=2$ it extends to include second-layer nodes (e.g., $x_{23}$), and so on.

**Key Distinction**: Like chain-of-thought training, this is a form of intermediate supervision, but it supervises a different object. CoT trains the model to generate a serialized token trace of the computation autoregressively, supervising the model in token space. In contrast, our latent space supervision method acts directly on the model's intermediate *latent states* within the forward pass, and supervises them to align with the step-by-step execution of the target algorithm. Notably, it requires a weaker supervision oracle than CoT. Beyond the final answer, our method needs only the depth of each node, which specifies the order in which nodes should be computed. However, while depth is available in our synthetic task, it may be unavailable or costly to obtain in other domains. Mechanism 2b removes this requirement by training the model to discover its own schedule without supervision.

</Callout>

<Callout title="Mechanism 2b: Self-Discovered Scheduling via Abstention" accent="accent">
**Motivation**: We propose a method to *learn* an incremental computation schedule without supervision from a ground-truth reference. As in Mechanism 2a, a loss is applied to the latent state after every recurrent iteration. Since there is no reference schedule defining the order in which different parts of the output should be computed, the objective itself must encourage incremental, sparse computation.

**Implementation**: We achieve this by allowing the model to **abstain** from making a prediction when it is uncertain, by predicting the $\langle \mathtt{empty} \rangle$ value. Abstaining receives partial credit so that it is preferred to making an incorrect prediction. This encourages the model to predict the output when it can do so reliably in the current iteration, and to abstain otherwise, deferring the node to a later iteration. For $\alpha \in [0, 1)$, a parameter defining the amount of partial credit received for abstaining, and letting $p_i^{(t)} = \mathrm{softmax}(W_{\mathtt{value}} \, E_i^{(t)})$ denote the predicted distribution over values for $x_i$ at iteration $t$, we define the self-scheduling loss as

$$
\mathrm{SelfSchedulingLoss} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{i \in [n]} - \log \big( p_i^{(t)}[\mathrm{Value}(x_i)] + \alpha \, p_i^{(t)}[\langle \mathtt{empty} \rangle] \big)
$$

As with standard cross-entropy loss, a confident correct value gives a loss near zero, and a confident, incorrect value gives a loss that grows without bound. Abstaining, on the other hand, gives a fixed loss of $-\log \alpha$ for $\alpha > 0$. Thus, abstaining becomes the optimal choice when the model is uncertain, and $\alpha$ sets the level of confidence required to commit. Since $\alpha < 1$, predicting the correct value is always preferred to abstaining, so the model is pushed to eventually fill in every node.

**Halting**: The abstention mechanism also provides a natural halting rule. At inference, we stop at the first iteration at which the model has committed to a value for every node:

$$
T_{\text{complete}}(X) := \min \{ t : \hat v_i^{(t)} \neq \langle \mathtt{empty} \rangle \ \forall i \}
$$

This halting mechanism adapts the iteration count to the input, and requires no knowledge of the input's depth at inference time.

</Callout>

<Callout title="Mechanism 3: Anchoring Latent Representations via Discretization" accent="accent">
**Motivation**: Recurrent models can suffer from representational drift during extended out-of-distribution computation. When processing significantly more iterations than seen during training, continuous representations gradually deviate from the learned manifold, causing performance degradation.

**Implementation**: We introduce a discretization mechanism that anchors the model's latent representations. After each recurrent iteration, continuous hidden states are projected into a structured discrete symbolic space with factored components (token syntax, variable identity, operation type, numerical value). These discrete states are then re-embedded to form input for the next iteration.

**The Discrete Space Structure** — consider the token sequence $17 = x_{42}$ `[sep]`:

| Token | → | syntax | variable | operation | value |
|:-----:|:-:|:------:|:--------:|:---------:|:-----:|
| `17` | → | `value` | `N/A` | `N/A` | `17` |
| `=` | → | `=` | `N/A` | `N/A` | `N/A` |
| $x_{42}$ | → | `variable` | $x_{42}$ | `N/A` | `empty` |
| `[sep]` | → | `[sep]` | `N/A` | `N/A` | `N/A` |

Note that the `value` factor of variable tokens (e.g., `x₄₂`) is `empty` initially. As the model processes recurrently, it iteratively computes values and updates this factor.

**Effect**: This discrete bottleneck ensures each iteration operates on representations from a shared, anchored space, preventing drift and enabling stable processing across many iterations.

**Training**: Because argmax decoding is not differentiable, we train the discretized model with teacher forcing across iterations, providing the correct intermediate state as the input to each iteration. These intermediate states are the targets of Mechanism 2a, so our implementation of discretization relies on the depth-supervised schedule. Extending it to Mechanism 2b is a direction for future work.

</Callout>

<Callout title="Mechanism 4: Learning to Self-Correct" accent="accent">
**Motivation**: In sequential reasoning, errors at any step can propagate and compromise the entire solution. As problem complexity scales, the likelihood of encountering errors increases, limiting the ability to generalize to more complex instances.

**Implementation**: Under the depth-supervised schedule of Mechanism 2a, the model only ever sees *correct* intermediate states during training, so it never learns to recover from a mistake. We therefore train each recurrent update on the model's *own* intermediate states, which may contain errors. Each training step makes two passes: the first applies the recurrent block to the correct intermediate latent state and trains it to predict the next state; the second applies it to the state the model itself just predicted, training it to produce the following correct state. When that predicted state contains an error, the model learns to repair the error while continuing the computation. The scheme introduces no additional hyperparameters. This trains the model to:

1. **Detect** when previously-computed values are incorrect (its own mistakes)
2. **Correct** errors in subsequent steps before proceeding with dependent computations

**Empirical Finding**: Discrete LSS already generalizes far beyond the training regime, but its solve rate decays slowly as circuits grow, from 99.4% at $N = 32$ to 94.1% at $N = 128$, and becomes increasingly seed-dependent (89.8%–99.5% across five seeds at $N = 128$). Training the model to recover from its own errors largely removes both effects: Discrete LSS + EC attains a mean solve rate of 99.6% at $N = 128$, and the spread across seeds narrows to 98.8%–100%.

</Callout>

### Ablation Analysis: Understanding Each Mechanism's Contribution

Figure (a) above shows a systematic ablation study. By comparing methods that differ in which mechanisms they implement (see the earlier table), we can isolate each mechanism's contribution:

<Callout type="info" title="Key Findings">
- **Recurrence matters:** Recurrent models improve in-distribution learning versus feedforward ones, but recurrence alone does not enable OOD generalization.
- **Algorithmic supervision is critical:** Chain-of-Thought (token-space supervision) gives limited OOD gains (to ~N ≈ 48). Latent space supervision with a depth-supervised schedule (Continuous LSS) extends generalization substantially further.
- **Discretization prevents drift:** Continuous LSS degrades on much larger graphs; adding a discrete latent bottleneck (Discrete LSS) yields far greater robustness, solving 94.1% of instances at N = 128 (mean of five seeds).
- **Error correction adds robustness:** The complete method (Discrete LSS + EC) with self-correction attains the strongest, near-perfect OOD performance across test sizes (99.6% at N = 128; 98.8%–100% across five seeds).
- **A schedule can be discovered, not only supervised:** Self-Scheduled LSS receives no depth information during training, yet solves 69.3% of instances at N = 128 on average (best seed 98.6%), where the chain-of-thought and end-to-end baselines solve none.

**The Synergy Effect:** Each mechanism addresses a distinct failure mode; combined, they produce a robust algorithmic learner whose joint performance exceeds individual contributions.
</Callout>

### Discovering a Computation Schedule without Depth Supervision

The models discussed so far are all trained with a depth-supervised computation schedule. We now explore whether a model can discover an incremental computation schedule without supervision. **Self-Scheduled LSS** is a recurrent model with continuous latent states trained with the $\mathrm{SelfSchedulingLoss}$, which supervises every iteration with the final solution and lets the model defer a node by abstaining (Mechanisms 1 and 2b). The model receives no depth information during training. At inference, the model iterates recurrently until all nodes are predicted without abstentions, halting at $T_{\text{complete}}$.

Averaged over five training seeds, the model solves 92.5% (sd 11.2) of instances in distribution and 69.3% (sd 33.1) at $N = 128$, where the chain-of-thought and end-to-end baselines solve none. It is competitive with Continuous LSS despite receiving no depth supervision, although the seed-to-seed spread of both methods is large. The best seed solves 98.6% of instances at $N = 128$.

In every seed, the first-fill time grows linearly with depth ($R^2 \geq 0.98$), and this trend extrapolates from the training regime (depth at most $6$) to circuits of depth up to $19$. The model therefore discovers a schedule that is aligned with the depth structure of the circuit and performs a bounded amount of computation per iteration, without any depth supervision.

The discovered schedules need not coincide with the layer-by-layer schedule used for depth supervision. Three seeds discover a more efficient schedule that computes $1.6$ to $2.1$ layers per iteration on average, while the other two compute $0.8$ to $0.9$. This is consistent with the objective, which rewards computing each node as soon as it can be computed reliably, but no sooner. We observe that the three seeds with the faster schedules are also the three that generalize best.

While performance is less consistent across seeds than for Discrete LSS + EC, some seeds generalize almost perfectly, which demonstrates that the method is capable of learning a robust, generalizable solution. This suggests that reliability can be improved through better training and optimization. It also motivates combining the self-scheduling loss of Mechanism 2b with discretization and error correction to enhance out-of-distribution stability, perhaps by introducing discretization in a post-training stage.
